1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a type of social engineering where an attacker pretends to be a trusted person or organization to trick you into giving up information, money or access, or into installing malware.

Email

Phishing

Mass emails posing as banks, delivery firms or online services, asking you to click a link, open a file or "verify" your account.

Targeted

Spear phishing

Personalized messages that use your name, job, colleagues or recent activity to look believable.

Executives

Whaling / BEC

Impersonating a CEO or supplier to push urgent payments or sensitive data. Also called business email compromise.

SMS

Smishing

Text messages about parcels, bank alerts or prizes with a short link.

Phone

Vishing

Calls from a "bank", "tech support" or "tax office" pressuring you to share codes or install software.

Web

Clone and fake sites

Look-alike login pages built to capture your username, password and one-time codes.

3. Recognize fake websites

Read a web address from the right side of the domain towards the left. The real owner is the registered domain, which sits just before the first single slash. The examples below use a fictional bank.

https://login.examplebank.com/signinLEGITIMATE: examplebank.com owns the "login" subdomain.
https://examplebank.com.verify-login.top/secureFAKE: the real domain is verify-login.top. "examplebank.com" is just a decoy subdomain.
https://examp1ebank.comFAKE: look-alike spelling. The letter "l" was swapped for the digit "1". Also watch for "rn" posing as "m".
https://examplebank.com@198.51.100.7/loginFAKE: everything before the "@" is ignored. The browser actually goes to the IP address.
The padlock does not mean "safe". It only means the connection is encrypted. Attackers get HTTPS certificates for fake sites too. Always check the domain itself.

Before you log in

Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark. Don't follow links from messages to sign-in pages.

Hover first

On a computer, hover over links to preview the destination. On mobile, press and hold the link.

Let tools help

A password manager only fills credentials on the exact saved domain, which is a strong clue when it refuses to autofill.

4. Social engineering tactics

Attackers exploit emotions more than technology. Learn the triggers and you will notice the pressure.

Urgency

"Act now or lose access"

Deadlines stop you from thinking. Real organizations rarely demand instant action through a link.

Authority

"This is your CEO / the police"

People obey perceived authority. Verify unusual requests through a second, known channel.

Fear

"Your account was hacked"

Scare tactics push you to click first and think later.

Greed

"You won a prize / refund"

If it is unexpected and too good to be true, it usually is.

Curiosity

"Is this you in the video?"

Intriguing subjects and unexpected shared files tempt you to open them.

Trust

Familiar names and context

Attackers copy logos, writing style and real conversation threads. Compromised accounts of people you know can send phishing too.

5. Real-world examples

Phishing is behind some of the best-known security incidents. Details are summarized from public reporting.

2013: Target. Attackers reportedly got in using credentials stolen from a third-party HVAC contractor, which started with a phishing email. The breach exposed payment card data for tens of millions of customers.
2016: US election campaign emails. A spear-phishing message disguised as a Google security alert tricked a campaign official into entering his password on a fake page, leading to a large email leak.
2017: Fake "Google Docs" invitation. A widespread campaign asked users to grant access to a malicious app that looked like a shared document, then spread by emailing the victim's contacts. Google shut it down within hours.
2020: Twitter. Attackers used phone-based social engineering (vishing) on employees to gain internal tools access, then took over high-profile accounts to promote a cryptocurrency scam.

Lesson: attackers go after people, including suppliers, because people are often easier to fool than systems.

6. Best practices to stay safe

  • Pause and verify. Contact the sender via a number or website you already trust.
  • Check the sender's real address, not just the display name.
  • Don't click or open unexpected links and attachments, especially double extensions like .pdf.exe.
  • Never share passwords or one-time codes. Real support never asks for them.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere. Authenticator apps or security keys beat SMS codes.
  • Use a password manager and unique passwords for each account.
  • Keep software, browser and antivirus updated.
  • Be careful with public Wi-Fi and don't log in to sensitive accounts on shared computers.
  • Report suspicious messages to your IT/security team or the service being impersonated.
  • When in doubt, don't. A delay costs nothing; a mistake can cost a lot.

What to do if you already clicked

  1. Don't panic and don't reply. Disconnect from the network if you opened a suspicious file.
  2. Change the affected password right away, from a device you trust, and change it anywhere you reused it.
  3. Enable multi-factor authentication and sign out of other sessions.
  4. Run a security scan and tell your IT/security team or your bank immediately.
  5. Watch accounts for unusual activity and report the phishing message.
